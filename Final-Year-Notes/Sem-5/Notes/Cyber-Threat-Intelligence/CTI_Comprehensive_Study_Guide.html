1. Introduction to Threat Intelligence

1.1 The Paradigm of Cyber Threat Intelligence

CTI Definition: The rigorous process of aggregating, transforming, analyzing, interpreting, and enriching data to provide necessary context for decision-making processes.

Primary Objective: Reduction of uncertainty - moving from “what is happening?” to “who is targeting me, why, and how?“

1.2 Threat Information vs. Threat Intelligence

Threat InformationThreat Intelligence
Raw artifacts (IP, hash, domain)Analyzed and contextualized data
Lacks contextAnswers “so what?”
Useful for immediate blockingSupports strategic decisions
Example: IP scanning firewallExample: IP belongs to nation-state actor targeting energy sector

1.3 Types of Cyber Threat Information

Indicators of Compromise (IoCs)

TypeDescriptionOperational UseVolatility
AtomicIP addresses, email addresses, file hashesAutomated blocking at firewallsHigh - easily changed
ComputedHash values, regex patternsIdentify specific malwareHigh - trivial to alter
BehavioralPatterns of activity (API calls, traffic patterns)Detect anomalous actionsLow - harder to change

Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs)

  • Tactics: High-level goals (Initial Access, Lateral Movement, Exfiltration)
  • Techniques: Specific methods (Spearphishing, Credential Dumping)
  • Procedures: Detailed implementation specific to threat group

IMPORTANT

TTPs are high-value intelligence because changing behavior is costly for adversaries compared to changing infrastructure.

Security Alerts and Advisories

Sources: US-CERT, ISACs, commercial vendors

  • Trigger vulnerability management workflows
  • Prompt patching before exploit becomes widespread

Threat Intelligence Reports

  • Human-readable prose documents
  • Describe threat actor profiles, ongoing campaigns
  • Provide situational awareness to leadership (CISOs, Board)

Tool Configurations

  • Snort signatures for IDS
  • YARA rules for malware hunting
  • ACLs for firewalls
  • Bridge intelligence to operations

1.4 Cross-Organizational Information Sharing

Benefits:

  • Shared Situational Awareness: Broader view beyond own network
  • Accelerated Response: Deploy countermeasures before being targeted
  • Cost Reduction: Avoid duplication of analysis effort

Challenges:

  • Trust and Privacy: Fear of exposing vulnerabilities, revealing PII
  • Standardization: Need for common formats (STIX) and protocols (TAXII)
  • Reputational Risk: Admission of breach may damage brand
  • Legal Barriers: GDPR vs. sectoral approach differences

1.5 Role of Nation-States

Dual Role:

  1. Threat Actors: APTs conducting espionage, sabotage
  2. Enablers: Establish NCSCs, CERTs for coordination

Legal Frameworks:

  • CISA (US): Liability protection for voluntary sharing
  • NIS Directive (EU): Mandatory sharing for critical infrastructure

2. Attack Scenarios and Threat Actors

2.1 Evolving Concepts

CategoryMotivationExamples
CybercrimeFinancial gainRansomware, fraud
CyberwarGeopolitical dominanceCritical infrastructure attacks
HacktivismIdeological beliefsAnonymous campaigns

Hybrid Threats: Cybercrime-as-a-Service (CaaS) where states purchase from criminals for deniability.

2.2 Emerging Trends

Cloud-Conscious Adversaries

  • Example: Scattered Spider
  • Exploit cloud-native features, IdPs, cloud policies
  • Abuse legitimate APIs for exfiltration
  • Target hybrid architecture “seams”

Identity-Based Attacks

  • Shift from malware to credential theft
  • Social engineering, phishing, infostealer logs
  • “Login, don’t break in” to bypass perimeter defenses

Supply Chain Exploitation

  • Examples: SolarWinds, Kaseya
  • Compromise trusted vendor to access thousands downstream
  • Weaponize software updates

7 Sequential Steps (Lockheed Martin):

  1. Reconnaissance: OSINT, Shodan, Google Dorks, email harvesting
  2. Weaponization: Couple RAT with deliverable (malicious macro in PDF)
  3. Delivery: Spear-phishing, malicious links, watering holes, USB drops
  4. Exploitation: Trigger vulnerability (software bug or human error)
  5. Installation/Initial Intrusion: Establish persistence (registry keys, scheduled tasks)
  6. Command and Control (C2): Beacon to controller, lateral movement
  7. Actions on Objectives: Data exfiltration, encryption (ransom), destruction

TIP

Defensive Doctrine: Breaking the chain at any single stage prevents attack success.

2.4 Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs)

Characteristics:

  • Persistence: Long-term access (months/years)
  • Sophistication: Custom tooling, zero-days
  • Resources: State-sponsored or affiliated
  • Low-and-Slow: Stealthy operations to avoid detection
  • Specific Targeting: Strategic entities (defense, government, energy, finance)

Examples:

  • Lazarus (North Korea): GhostCall, GhostHire campaigns against Web3
  • Tick (China): Long-term espionage with LotL tactics

3. Monitoring, Logging, and Network Analysis

3.1 Data Sources for Intelligence Extraction

Log Data

  • Authentication Logs: Brute-force, credential stuffing, anomalous logins
  • Application Logs: SQL injection attempts, database queries
  • Endpoint Logs (EDR): Process spawning, file mods, registry changes, memory manipulation

Network Traffic

  • Full Packet Capture (PCAP):
    • Gold standard for forensics
    • Captures payload if unencrypted
    • Expensive to store
  • Flow Data (NetFlow):
    • Summary: Who, What, Where, When, How Much
    • No content capture
    • Essential for high-volume networks
    • Useful for DDoS detection, large exfiltration

3.2 Analysis Techniques

  1. Signature-Based Detection

    • Compare against known threat patterns
    • High precision, low false positives
    • Limitation: Fails against zero-days

  2. Anomaly Detection

    • Establish baseline of “normal” behavior
    • Flag deviations (unusual data volume, new ports)
    • Critical for zero-day and insider threats

  3. Correlation

    • Link disparate events from different sources
    • Example: Failed login → Successful login → New admin account → Large file transfer = Breach
    • Tool: SIEM systems (Splunk, QRadar)

4. Information Sharing Architecture

  1. Efficient Cooperation

    • Define what to share (IoCs, vulnerabilities, incidents)
    • Define who to share with (sector peers, government, supply chain)
    • Trust is the currency

  2. Legal and Regulatory Landscape

    • NIS Directive (EU): Mandatory sharing for critical infrastructure
    • GDPR: Limits on sharing personal data
    • CISA (US): Liability protection for voluntary sharing

  3. Standardization Efforts

    • STIX: Structured Threat Information Expression - language to describe threats
    • TAXII: Trusted Automated eXchange - transport protocol for STIX over HTTPS
    • IODEF: XML format for incident data

  4. Regional Implementation

    • ENISA (EU), FIRST, NATO CCD CoE
    • APCERT (Asia-Pacific)

  5. Technology Integration

    • Threat Intelligence Platforms (TIPs)
    • SIEM integration
    • Automated feed ingestion

4.2 Technical Standards

STIX + TAXII:

  • STIX describes: “This IP (Indicator) → associated with this Malware → uses this Vulnerability → by this Threat Actor (Campaign)”
  • TAXII delivers STIX packages from Producer to Consumer
  • Enables machine-to-machine sharing at scale

5. CTI Communities

5.1 Community Models

FeatureHub-and-SpokePeer-to-Peer (P2P)
StructureCentral authority aggregates/redistributesDecentralized mesh network
ExamplesNational CERTs, Dutch NDNETIS CERT-SOC Telco
ProsCentralized filtering, easier for less mature membersNo single point of failure, high trust
ConsSingle point of failure, central entity sees all dataHarder to scale, requires mature capabilities

5.2 MISP (Malware Information Sharing Platform)

Functionality:

  • Store, correlate, and share IoCs
  • Input “Events” with “Attributes” (IPs, hashes, domains)
  • Automatic correlation across organizations
  • Integration with IDS (Snort/Suricata) and SIEMs

Privacy Controls:

  • Traffic Light Protocol (TLP):
    • TLP:RED: Eyes-only, do not share
    • TLP:AMBER: Share within organization/community
    • TLP:GREEN: Share with partners
    • TLP:WHITE: Public disclosure

5.3 Case Studies

ETIS (European Telcos):

  • P2P model for telecom-specific threats
  • High trust among peers
  • Sector-specific sharing effectiveness

NDN (Dutch National Detection Network):

  • Hub-and-Spoke with government (NCSC) as hub
  • Shares with critical infrastructure
  • Privacy concerns managed through legal safeguards

6. Situational Awareness for Strategic Decision Making

6.1 Cyber Situational Awareness (CSA)

Definition: Perception of elements in environment, comprehension of meaning, projection of future status.

CSA Focus: Know assets, understand threats, current network state, predict attack impact.

6.2 Situational Awareness Models

Endsley’s Three-Level Model

  1. Level 1 (Perception): Gathering raw data (logs, alerts, traffic) - “What is happening?”
  2. Level 2 (Comprehension): Understanding significance through correlation - “What does it mean?”
  3. Level 3 (Projection): Predicting future states - “What will happen next?”

OODA Loop (Boyd)

  • Observe: Collect logs, traffic, external intel
  • Orient: Analyze against threat intelligence and context (most critical)
  • Decide: Choose mitigation strategy
  • Act: Implement decision

Goal: Complete OODA loop faster than adversary

JDL Data Fusion Model

  • Level 1: Object Assessment
  • Level 2: Situation Assessment
  • Level 3: Impact Assessment

6.3 National Level Situational Awareness

  • Aggregate data from all critical sectors (Energy, Finance, Telecom, Transport)
  • NCSCs as fusion centers
  • Create “Common Operational Picture” (COP)
  • Distinguish isolated crime from coordinated nation-state attacks

7. Legal Implications of Information Sharing

7.1 EU Cybersecurity Legal Framework

NIS Directive (Network and Information Security)

Requirements for OES and DSPs:

  1. Take appropriate technical/organizational security measures
  2. Report serious incidents to CSIRTs without undue delay

Impact: Mandatory sharing overrides non-disclosure concerns

GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)

Conflict: IP addresses, usernames, emails in phishing headers = personal data

Solution: Recital 49 recognizes processing personal data for network security as “legitimate interest” if:

  • Strictly necessary
  • Proportionate to purpose

WARNING

Sharing must be proportionate - controlled sharing via MISP is defensible; public Pastebin dump violates proportionality.

7.2 Legal Barriers

  • Legal Certainty: Unclear what is permitted
  • Cross-Border: Varying national implementations
  • Dynamic vs. Static IPs: Different sensitivity interpretations

8. Legal Case Studies

8.1 Distribution of Security-Relevant Information with Personal Data

Scenario: Organization wants to share botnet-infected IP list

Analysis:

  • IPs are personal data under GDPR
  • Options:
    1. Anonymize (hash IPs - reduces utility)
    2. Rely on “legitimate interest” (Recital 49)
  • Controlled MISP sharing: Legal ✓
  • Public Pastebin: Violates proportionality ✗

8.2 Harm to Reputation of Third Parties

Scenario: Incorrect attribution (claim Company X hosts malware)

Analysis:

  • False intelligence → defamation lawsuit, commercial damages
  • Mitigation:
    • Attach Confidence Levels
    • Verify data before dissemination
    • Use TLP:AMBER to contain damage vs. public blog

8.3 Information Leakage of Threat Intelligence

Scenario: TLP:RED zero-day details leaked to press

Analysis:

  • NDA violations
  • If hackers exploit before patch: negligence, aiding cybercrime
  • Controls: Strict access controls, audit logs, attribution

8.4 Harm from Disproportionate Mitigation

Scenario: Automated block of IP range disrupts hospital services

Analysis:

  • Active defense carries liability
  • Disproportionate/negligent targeting → liable for collateral damage
  • Mitigation: Human-in-the-loop for high-impact decisions

8.5 Service Provider Legal Implications

Scenario: CSP detects malicious traffic from customer instance

Analysis:

  • SLA/ToS dictate duties
  • NIS2: Reporting obligations for CSPs
  • Balance: Duty of care vs. customer privacy (DPI may violate wiretapping laws)

9.1 Introduction

ECOSSIAN: European Control System Security Incident Analysis Network

  • EU FP7 research project
  • Pan-European system for Critical Infrastructure protection
  • Local detection → international response

ECOSSIAN proposed a hierarchical, three-layered architecture to manage the flow of intelligence and incident reports.

  1. O-SOC (Operator Security Operation Center)

    • Local SOC at critical facility (power plant, bank)
    • Role: Local detection, initial triage, data sanitization
    • Sends incident reports to national level

  2. N-SOC (National Security Operation Center)

    • Government-run centralized body
    • Role: Strategic correlation across sectors
    • Detects cross-sector campaigns
    • Issues early warnings and mitigation advice

  3. E-SOC (European Security Operation Center)

    • Pan-European entity
    • Connects N-SOCs across member states
    • Handles cross-border threats

9.3 Roles and Responsibilities

  • CI Operators (O-SOC): Local defense, sensor tuning, anonymization
  • National Analysts (N-SOC): Maintain national SA, issue actionable advisories
  • Standardized Processes: IODEF, STIX for interoperability

9.4 Application Cases

  1. Early Warning in Finance: Detect coordinated DDoS across banks, warn others
  2. Gas Distribution: Identify SCADA-targeting malware in pipelines
  3. Pan-European Interdependency: Track threat cascading from energy to telecom

9.5 Lessons Learned

IMPORTANT

  • Trust is Fragile: Build on established relationships
  • Standardization is Key: Uniform formats prevent data overload
  • Privacy by Design: Anonymization at source (O-SOC) ensures GDPR compliance and private sector participation

Key Takeaways for Exam

IMPORTANT

  1. Distinguish Threat Information (raw data) from Threat Intelligence (contextualized analysis)
  2. Understand the Cyber Kill Chain and how defensive interventions work at each stage
  3. Master the five dimensions of information sharing architecture
  4. Know STIX/TAXII technical standards and their purpose
  5. Comprehend legal frameworks: NIS Directive, GDPR Recital 49, CISA
  6. Apply TLP classifications appropriately
  7. Explain ECOSSIAN’s three-layered architecture (O-SOC, N-SOC, E-SOC)
