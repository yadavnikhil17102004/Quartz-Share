CTI Chapter 7: Regulations and Standards

Source Material

  • Chapter 7.pptx

Introduction

Sharing information is vital for security, but it faces legal hurdles. This chapter focuses on the EU’s Cybersecurity Legal Framework, particularly the NIS Directive and GDPR.

The Conflict

We need to share data to stop hackers, but laws (like privacy and trade secrets) often restrict what we can share.

EU Cybersecurity Legal Framework

The EU has evolved its strategy from basic infrastructure protection to comprehensive cybersecurity laws.

Key Directives

DirectiveFocusKey Requirement
Cybercrime Directive (2013)Criminal LawDefines illegal access, interference, and mandates penalties.
ECI Directive (2008)Critical InfrastructureProtects Energy and Transport sectors.
NIS Directive (2016)Network SecurityMandates CSIRTs, National Strategies, and Incident Reporting for “Operators of Essential Services”.

NIS Directive Goals

  1. Capabilities: Every member state must have a CSIRT and a strategy.
  2. Cooperation: Cross-border collaboration via the CSIRT Network.
  3. Reporting: Mandatory incident reporting for critical sectors (Energy, Banking, Health, Transport).

Breach Notification Obligations

Laws now force companies to admit when they’ve been hacked.

  • Telecoms (2009): Must notify regulators of significant security breaches.
  • eIDAS (2014): Trust service providers must notify within 24 hours.
  • GDPR (2016): General Data Protection Regulation.

GDPR Article 33

Data breaches must be reported to the supervisory authority within 72 hours if they pose a risk to individuals.

Barriers to Sharing

1. Intellectual Property (IP) & Trade Secrets

Sharing threat info might reveal proprietary software details or business secrets.

  • Copyright: Protects databases and code.
  • Trade Secrets Directive (2016): Protects confidential business info. Sharing must be careful not to void these protections.

2. National Security

Information about Critical Infrastructure (CI) is often classified.

  • Classified Information: Rules vary by country (e.g., “Top Secret”, “Confidential”).
  • Barrier: Private companies often lack the security clearance to receive government intelligence.

3. Data Protection (GDPR)

The biggest hurdle. IP addresses can be personal data.

The Breyer Case (CJEU)

The Court ruled that Dynamic IP addresses are personal data if the ISP can identify the user. This means sharing IPs requires a legal basis under GDPR.

GDPR Roles in Sharing

  • Controller: Decides why and how data is processed (e.g., the victim organization).
  • Processor: Processes data on behalf of the controller (e.g., a cloud security provider).
  • Joint Controllers: Two entities deciding together (e.g., two banks sharing fraud data).

Lawful Grounds for Sharing

Under GDPR, you need a reason to process data:

  1. Legal Obligation: “The law says I must report this.”
  2. Public Interest: “This protects the nation.”
  3. Legitimate Interest: “Processing this IP is necessary to secure my network.” (Recital 49 of GDPR explicitly states network security is a legitimate interest).
