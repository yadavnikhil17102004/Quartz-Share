CTI Chapter 4: Threat Intelligence Sharing

  • Chapter 4.pptx

The Need for Information Sharing

Cyber threats cross national borders and exploit the interconnectedness of critical infrastructures. Effective defense requires collaboration.

Key Insight

“Sharing threat information aids in coordinated countermeasures and helps protect against various cyber threats like cybercrime, cyberwar, hacktivism, and terrorism.”

Dimensions of Information Sharing

The syllabus defines five key dimensions for effective sharing:

Dimension I: Efficient Cooperation and Coordination

  • Sharing IoCs, vulnerabilities, and zero-day exploits rapidly.
  • Coordinating responses to large-scale attacks (e.g., WannaCry).

Dimension II: Legal and Regulatory Landscape

  • Complying with laws (e.g., GDPR, NIS Directive, CISA).
  • Navigating liability and antitrust concerns.

Dimension III: Standardization Efforts

  • Using common formats (STIX, TAXII) for interoperability.
  • Adopting standard taxonomies (e.g., TLP).

Dimension IV: Regional and International Implementations

  • Integration of CERTs and national centers.
  • Cross-border cooperation (e.g., EU CSIRT Network).

Dimension V: Technology Integration

  • Implementing tools like MISP and SIEM.
  • Automating the ingestion of feeds into defensive tools (Firewalls/IDS).

Technical Standards and Protocols

Automation is key to handling the volume of threat data. Standards ensure machines can talk to each other.

OASIS Standards

The "Big Three"

  • STIX (Structured Threat Information eXpression): A language to represent threat info (Who, What, Where, Why).
  • TAXII (Trusted Automated eXchange of Indicator Information): A protocol to transport STIX data (How it is shared).
  • CybOX (Cyber Observable eXpression): A schema for describing observables (files, hashes, IPs). Note: Merged into STIX 2.0.

Traffic Light Protocol (TLP)

TLP: Controlling Sharing

TLP is a set of designations used to ensure that sensitive information is shared with the appropriate audience.

  • 🔴 TLP:RED: Not for disclosure, restricted to participants only.
  • 🟠 TLP:AMBER: Limited disclosure, restricted to participants’ organizations.
  • 🟢 TLP:GREEN: Limited disclosure, restricted to the community.
  • ⚪ TLP:CLEAR: Disclosure is not limited (Public).

Sharing Models

Different architectures suit different needs.

  • Hub-and-Spoke: Central authority (e.g., National CERT) manages data. Good for control, bad for single point of failure.
  • Peer-to-Peer (P2P): Direct trust between organizations. Faster, resilient, but harder to scale trust.
  • Source-Subscriber: Commercial feeds (e.g., antivirus vendors) pushing updates to clients.

IETF Standards

  • IODEF (Incident Object Description Exchange Format): XML format for sharing incident info (RFC 5070).
  • RID (Real-time Inter-network Defense): Protocol to transport IODEF (RFC 6545).

Tools for Sharing

MISP (Malware Information Sharing Platform)

  • Open-source platform for sharing IoCs and threat intelligence.
  • Supports correlation (finding links between events).
  • “Store, Share, Collaborate.”

OpenIOC

  • XML-based schema for describing technical characteristics of threats.
  • Created by Mandiant.

Regional and International Cooperation

  • ENISA: European Network and Information Security Agency. Promotes cooperation in EU.
  • NIST: US National Institute of Standards and Technology. Develops the Cybersecurity Framework.
  • FIRST: Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams. Global community of CERTs.
  • APCERT: Asia-Pacific Computer Emergency Response Team.

Key Takeaways

  • Need for Sharing: Cyber threats are global; isolation is vulnerability.
  • Standards: STIX (Language) and TAXII (Transport) are the gold standards.
  • Models: Hub-and-Spoke (Centralized) vs P2P (Trust-based).
  • Tools: MISP is the most popular open-source platform for sharing IoCs.
  • Regulation: GDPR and NIS Directive mandate reporting and sharing in specific contexts.
